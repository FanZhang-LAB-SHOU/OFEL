Mingfeng Jiang shows that ignoring death after spawning distorts squid stock assessments

A new model, ACLS, treats post-spawning death as part of squid population dynamics, and shows that ignoring it inflates estimates of spawning stock biomass.

Research updates

Lab members: Mingfeng JiangSisong DongFan Zhang

OFEL PhD student Mingfeng Jiang has developed a stock assessment model for squid and other semelparous species, animals that spawn once and die soon afterwards. The model builds death after spawning directly into the population dynamics, giving a more faithful picture of how these populations change over time. The study is published in the Journal of Applied Ecology.

Semelparous species matter for both ecosystems and fisheries: cephalopods and Pacific salmon together make up about 5% of global marine capture production. Yet most stock assessment models were designed for fish that spawn many times over their lives. When those models are applied to squid, they can misrepresent population dynamics and give a misleading view of stock status.

A model built around one spawning

The new model, ACLS (an age-structured statistical catch-at-length model for semelparity), removes mature animals from the population once they have spawned, so spent individuals are no longer counted in later time steps. Conventional models carry them forward as if they could spawn again.

Testing it on squid and salmon

Mingfeng and colleagues tested ACLS in simulations covering two contrasting semelparous life histories, short-lived squid and longer-lived Pacific salmon, under different fishing pressures, environmental variability and stock–recruitment relationships. ACLS consistently outperformed the conventional model, estimating population dynamics more accurately and with less bias. Sensitivity tests showed where care is needed: wrong assumptions about natural mortality before spawning, or about the timing of maturity, can reduce its performance.

The simulations also showed what goes wrong when post-spawning death is ignored. The conventional model overestimates biomass in the older age groups, above all the cohort that has already spawned, and this inflates estimates of spawning stock biomass.

Jumbo squid case study

Applied to jumbo squid (Dosidicus gigas), ACLS fitted the catch, abundance-index and length-composition data well and showed no evident retrospective bias. Its estimates of key management quantities differed markedly from those of four widely used alternatives: Stock Synthesis 3, the conventional ACL model, SPiCT and Catch-MSY.

Why it matters

The study's message is that population models can only support management credibly if they represent the life history of the species they describe. By accounting for death after spawning, ACLS gives a biologically grounded way to estimate stock status and management reference points for squid and other semelparous species, and a firmer basis for managing cephalopod fisheries sustainably.

Mingfeng Jiang and postdoctoral researcher Sisong Dong are co-first authors. Professor Fan Zhang and Professor Xinjun Chen are co-corresponding authors. The study was carried out with Professor André E. Punt's team at the University of Washington.
