Xin Cheng maps where and when Pacific tuna vessels drop off AIS tracking

Seven years of AIS records from 1,016 Pacific tuna vessels show that tracking gaps are shrinking, and pinpoint where vessels still go silent.

Research updates

Lab members: Xin ChengQinqin Lin

OFEL postdoctoral researcher Xin Cheng has led the first systematic assessment of vessel-tracking data quality for Pacific tuna longline and purse seine fleets. Analysing seven years of Automatic Identification System (AIS) records, the study shows that gaps in the data have fallen steadily, and that the places where vessels drop off the record have stayed the same while shrinking. It is published in npj Ocean Sustainability.

Why data gaps matter

Tuna are among the most valuable fish in the Pacific, and managing them depends on accurate fishing data and estimates of fishing effort. AIS has become a core source for studying vessel behaviour and mapping fishing grounds. Its signals can drop out, though: through equipment faults, signal congestion or patchy coverage, or because a crew switches the transponder off, sometimes to hide illegal fishing. These gaps had never been assessed systematically for Pacific tuna fleets, which limited how far AIS could be trusted in management decisions.

130 million positions, three thresholds

Xin Cheng and colleagues assembled about 130 million high-resolution AIS positions from 1,016 tuna longline and purse seine vessels fishing in the Pacific between 2017 and 2023. They defined an anomaly as a reporting gap, an interval between consecutive signals longer than a set threshold, and tested three thresholds: 60, 90 and 120 minutes. The approach turns data gaps from noise into something that can be counted and mapped.

What the data show

AIS gaps declined steadily from year to year, with no clear pattern by month or by hour of the day. Coverage now spans almost the entire Pacific fishing area. Gap hotspots stayed in the same places every year but grew smaller, and they are concentrated mainly in waters around Pacific island countries.

Purse seiners lose their signal more often than longliners, and their gaps are more tightly clustered, chiefly near Pacific island countries between about 5°N and 10°S and 140°E and 170°E. Longline hotspots are more scattered, including off northeastern Australia and in open waters of the south-central Pacific.

Why it matters

The results confirm that AIS data are usable, and improving, as a foundation for fisheries research, and they show where monitoring effort is most needed. The authors suggest that regional fisheries management organisations work with coastal states to target the persistent hotspots around Pacific island countries, and that AIS gaps be tracked routinely as a basis for differentiated, data-driven oversight of fishing fleets.

Xin Cheng is the first author. Lecturer Qinqin Lin and Professor Xinjun Chen are co-corresponding authors.
